# SANKET, explained like you are five

Imagine a big bank with a post room. Every day, thousands of letters arrive from customers.
Each letter needs to go to the right desk: the card desk, the fraud desk, the money-transfer desk, and so on.
SANKET is a helper in that post room.

## 1. The door

A letter arrives. The helper first checks it is a real letter.
Is there anything written on it? Is it a sensible size? Does it have words, not just squiggles?
If not, it goes straight back with a note saying what was wrong.

## 2. The black marker

Some customers write their card number, phone number, email or Aadhaar number in the letter.
The helper takes a black marker and covers them up, writing [CARD] or [PHONE] instead.
For card numbers there is a little arithmetic trick that real card numbers always pass.
If a long number fails the trick, it is probably an order number, so the helper leaves it alone.
Only the covered-up letter is ever kept.

## 3. Reading the letter

The helper has read about seven thousand old letters, each already marked with what the customer wanted.
From them it learned which words point to which wish.
"Arrived", "card" and "waiting" point towards "where is my new card?".
"Not me", "payment" and "don't recognise" point towards "someone used my card".
For a new letter, the helper adds up these clues for each of 77 possible wishes and says how sure it is about each.

## 4. Showing its working

A good helper does not just say "this is a card letter".
It points at the exact words that made it think so, and how much each word counted.
That way a person can check the helper's reasoning in a second.

## 5. Choosing the desk

There is a simple chart on the wall: each of the 77 wishes belongs to one of 7 desks, and is either urgent, normal or not urgent.
The project's team wrote this chart. It did not come with the letters.
If the helper is sure which desk a letter belongs to, it drops it straight in that desk's tray.
If it is not sure, or the letter is full of words it has never seen (for example, a letter in Hinglish),
it puts the letter in a special tray for a person to look at.
If there is even a fair chance that the letter is about fraud or a lost card, it gets a red "urgent" sticker,
because a missed fraud letter is much worse than an extra look.

## 6. The person at the special tray

A person picks up letters from the special tray, urgent ones first, then the oldest.
They either agree with the helper or write the right wish.
Their name, the time and the answer go into a logbook.
Two people cannot both answer the same letter: the second one is told "already done".

## 7. Watching for change

Every so often the helper compares today's letters with the letters it learned from.
Are they much longer? Full of unfamiliar words? Is the helper less sure than usual?
Are far more letters going to one desk?
If things look very different, a light turns red. That does not mean the helper is wrong.
It means someone should look.

## 8. Learning again, carefully

The logbook of corrections becomes new practice material.
A new helper is trained with the old letters plus the corrections.
Before it replaces the old helper, both take the same locked exam that neither has ever seen.
The new one only gets the job if it scores at least as well (within a hair).
If someone sneaks wrong answers into the logbook, the exam and a sanity check catch it and the new helper is turned away.

## What the helper is not good at

It learned from short English questions, so it struggles with Hinglish and long, messy letters.
When it struggles, it usually asks a person instead of guessing.
In a test with 60 Hinglish letters, 26 went to a person, none went to the wrong desk on their own,
and 1 went to the right desk with the wrong wish written on it.
